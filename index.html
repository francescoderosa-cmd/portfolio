What Cabinet approved in its place

  1. 01

    Application, on a phone

    Nine days of paper intake replaced by mobile data collection in KoBoToolbox, filled in at the community centre instead of carried to the ministry.

  2. 02

    Eligibility, checked against data already held

    Seventeen days of manual verification across nine steps. BMIS already holds case management; the gap is adoption, not software.

    Fifteen steps and fourteen days to approve one list. E-signatures and digital paysheet transmission remove the courier runs between offices.

The real challenge, missing data

You can't map hunger with empty widgets.

The old HungerMap often showed nothing. Each country office uploads only the data it needs locally, so when capacity lagged whole countries came up blank, on a platform built to show where hunger is worst.

How we solved it, across data, product and design

01 · At the data level

A baseline that's always populated

Working directly with VAM officers in each country and the data scientists and analysts at HQ, we defined a data-level key so a core set of indicators is always present, closing the information holes.

02 · At the product level

Every widget can be switched on or off

External and internal widgets are fully customizable and toggleable, so data teams and developers surface only the widgets that actually hold data. The platform never shows an empty box.

03 · At the design level

A public view built on people, not figures

With the same VAM and HQ data teams, we chose a storytelling approach for the external audience, always centring on the people in need rather than raw numbers.

The challenge

Register the hardest-to-reach, from a phone.

Registering people in person is slow, costly and often impossible in an emergency, and cloned pages put vulnerable people at risk. The portal had to work remotely, on a weak connection, for someone filling a form for the first time.

Three constraints shaped every decision

01 · Access

In-person didn't scale

Queues, staff and offices can't keep pace with the speed and size of a crisis. People needed to register from their own phone, wherever they were.

02 · Trust & safety

Cloned pages, real risk

Fraudulent look-alike pages exploited people in need. The real portal had to be unmistakably WFP, secure and duplicate-proof.

03 · Everyone

Every level of digital literacy

The same form had to be usable by someone confident online and by someone who had never filled one, on any device, in their own language.

Discovery, at one remove

We could not test with the people who would use it.

Sessions with people in crisis were not possible. The research came from the two places that were: the colleagues who had already run the first version in the field, and the evidence on how people with little digital experience actually deal with a form.

01 · Field feedback

The people who had already shipped it

Colleagues who implemented the Self Registration App in country were the closest proxy available: what they watched fail at distribution points set the priorities for the form.

02 · Desk research

Low digital literacy meets a long form

I researched how people with little digital experience behave with forms and dropdown selectors: where they stall, what they misread, what makes them abandon.

03 · The honest limit

Second-hand evidence, named as such

Without sessions with the people registering, the evidence is indirect. The field colleagues were the check: every decision went back to those who had watched the first version in use.

The core of the work, accessible forms

One form, for every level of digital literacy.

Registration is one long, high-stakes form: name, household, roles, location, identity, bank details. I rebuilt it around choices instead of typing, honest “I don’t have this” paths and constant guidance, so nothing about a person’s situation locks them out.

Identity, your way

Four ways to identify yourself

National ID, passport, driving licence or UNHCR ID, so displaced and undocumented people are never turned away.

Tap, don't type

Family roles as buttons

Household roles and status are tappable options, not free text, removing spelling, language and keyboard barriers.

No wrong answer

"I don't have a phone / email / bank account"

Explicit paths for people with no phone, email or bank account, plus a simple 6-digit PIN instead of passwords.

Digital financial inclusion, two journeys

From a shared phone in Somalia
to a wallet of her own in Jordan.

The same research method, applied in two very different mobile money contexts. Each journey surfaced findings that shaped how WFP designs digital payments for women.

The list on the mosque wall. Selection was announced where the community already looks, and illustrated sessions worked for people who cannot read.
Payments cannot go to a husband's SIM. Aamiina needed a card in her own name, so getting women their own SIM became part of the service.
The shopkeeper is the interface. Checking messages, cashing out and paying all pass through trusted humans, not through the app alone.
Info sessions turn worry into confidence. After one session Hana could compare providers, fees and limits, and chose her own wallet.
Dormant wallets are a real failure path. Her brother's payment bounced because his unused wallet had been deactivated, reactivation had to be designed for.
Agents work, shops do not yet. Cash-out is easy nearby, but local shops still refuse mobile money, the last mile of acceptance is the next problem.

What the Ukraine response established

Registration without an ID. Online self-registration accepted Drina when every help desk had turned her away.
An SMS as proof. A text message became her identity token for assistance, even where banks still refused it.
A voucher fallback. When no ID meant no cash transfer, supermarket vouchers kept the family fed and bought medicine.
An emergency playbook. The case fed WFP's CBT emergency tips: backup transfer plans, minimal data in the first 90 days, ready answers for people without documents.
Design principles, what this research taught us

Six rules for designing
in extreme contexts.

1

Design for the margin

If it works for someone who cannot read, walk far or own a phone, it works for everyone. Design for the hardest case first.

2

Communicate proactively

Waiting without news erodes trust faster than bad news. Confirm every step, even when nothing has changed.

3

No ID ≠ No cash

People fleeing rarely carry documents. Every service needs a pathway that works without official identification.

4

Proxy access matters

Phones, literacy and mobility are often shared family resources. Design for use through someone the person trusts.

5

Dignity in disbursement

How money is handed over matters as much as how much. Privacy at the cash point is not a luxury.

6

Offline-first always

Connectivity, devices and electricity come and go. The service must keep its promise when the network does not.

WFP cash operations require managing complex knowledge (processes, guidelines, responsibilities) and collaborative project tracking simultaneously. Discovery, focus groups and interviews

The idea came from the country offices, not from a brief.

Focus groups and interviews with colleagues in almost every country office running cash distributions. Three things came back from them, and between them they defined the product.

01 · The need

Named by the people doing the work

Not a need inferred from a brief. Field officers described what a distribution actually costs them, step by step, in their own offices.

02 · The as-is

What was wrong with the material they had

The documents in use were scattered across formats and impossible to consult in the field. The interviews mapped exactly where they broke down.

03 · The hope

The same ask, office after office

Something more digital that would shorten and speed up the distribution process. It came back from nearly every country office we spoke to.

The challenge

Inherit a look, earn a behaviour.

A shared corporate concept is a gift and a cage: it guarantees family resemblance but was never drawn around album art, playlists and a docked player. TIMmusic had to look TIM and behave like a music service.

01 · The constraint

One identity, four services

The visual concept was shared with TIMvision, TIMgames and TIMreading. Music got its own declination, not its own language.

02 · The content

A catalogue, not a catalogue page

Editorial playlists and personal ones, radio, offline listening, recommendations, charts and artist pages: every surface competes for the same screen.

03 · The proof

Method, before pixels

The client's request asked for architecture and interaction thinking first, and only then a visual mockup that proved the concept could sing.

Discovery, category benchmark

Benchmark first, then the brand.

A shared corporate concept arrived with the brief, drawn for a family of services rather than for music. Before bending it into a streaming product I benchmarked the category: how the services people already use organise a library, where they keep the player, what a listener reaches for without being taught. Those reference points, not the concept, decided the structure.

The request, answered

Three asks, three deliverables.

The client's request for information was precise about what it wanted to see. Each ask mapped to a piece of work, and each is visible in the screens below.

Ask 01 · Architecture

First and second level architecture: twelve sections, from onboarding and search to charts, genres and the personal library, with a persistent right rail for playlists.

Ask 02 · Interactions

Wireframes for creating a playlist and editing the queue, the two moments where a streaming service feels effortless or falls apart: an inline edit mode with undo, restore and confirm.

Ask 03 · Visual proof

A mockup that creates a playlist and plays a track, delivered at full fidelity: cover art drives the page palette, the player stays docked while you browse.

The challenge

Say "virus" without saying "panic".

Health content defaults to clinical and frightening. For pregnant women looking up a virus that can harm a baby, that is the fastest way to lose them: the app had to be warm enough to read and precise enough to trust.

01 · Tone

A friendly face for a scary word

An illustrated character, soft coral gradients and short plain sentences turn "cytomegalovirus" into something you can actually read while worried.

02 · Two visitors

One door, two journeys

Anyone can read prevention information freely. Women in a monitoring programme enter a code and unlock a private space built around their weeks.

03 · Usefulness

Beyond the leaflet

A weekly calculator, an agenda for appointments and the nearest testing centres, with distance, phone and email one tap away.

Discovery, benchmark and subject matter

A medical subject you cannot get wrong.

Cytomegalovirus is technical ground, and the audience is reading it while worried. Three strands of research came before the first screen.

01 · Benchmark

How health apps handle sensitive data

I studied how applications built around sensitive health data are set up: what they ask for, what they protect, and where they draw the line between open information and a private area.

02 · Subject matter

Sessions with the association, doctors included

The association that commissioned the app has doctors among its members. Recurring sessions with them checked the content and the claims: on a medical subject accuracy is a design constraint, not a proofread at the end.

03 · Illustration

What a drawing is allowed to do here

I researched which register of illustration holds up in a sensitive health context: warm enough to be read by someone frightened, never so playful that it makes light of the diagnosis.

The architecture

Where the private side begins.

The navigation tree that set the product: everything above the dotted line is open to anyone, everything below it needs a code. Drawing that line first is what kept the public side generous and the private side focused. Click to zoom.

The design

Coral, not clinical.

The delivered design: entry screen, menu, information pages and the nearby centres list, with a custom icon set drawn for the app. Click the board to zoom.
